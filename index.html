Why pay for this when I can just ask ChatGPT?

You can, and for the big names it works fine. The problem is the small ones. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini answer from data that can be months old, so a startup which launched this spring, or the one that is working silently at the same scale as you, usually is not there. Those are exactly the competitors a small business needs to know about.

We don't rely on what a model remembers. We keep our own database of more than 300,000 startup domains and we crawl it actively, so what you get is what is on their websites now.

Is the report written by AI?

Partly, and we prefer to be honest about it. The gathering is automated: we built an agentic system and a RAG on top of everything we collect, because no human can read thousands of pages every month.

But the final document is always checked by a specialist before it reaches you. If a number looks wrong or a conclusion is weak, it gets fixed or removed. So there is no AI slop in your documents.

Where does your data come from?

From three places. First, the listing websites where startups publish themselves: Product Hunt, G2, AlternativeTo and more than 100 other directories.

Second, beside the listings we also check the VCs, accelerators, angel investors and incubators of the top 50 countries: the USA, Canada, Australia, the UK, the Netherlands, Germany, the UAE, Türkiye, China and 40 more. From all of these we built a database of over 300,000 unique domains, one per startup.

Third, the competitors' own websites, which we check at regular intervals for changes in pricing, products, hiring, blog and social media. On top of that our crawler keeps indexing new startups as they appear, so the list is never frozen.

What do I actually do with the report?

Use it for planning. The best moment to read it is before you plan the next sprint or change a price. You see what the others charge, what they shipped last month and who they are hiring, and from that you decide what to build next and what to leave.

The point is to stop spending time and money on moves the market didn't ask for.

Competitor analysis, explained

What is competitive intelligence as a service?

Competitive intelligence is the work of collecting public information about your competitors and your market, and turning it into something you can decide with. It covers things like pricing, product changes, hiring, traffic and funding. Big companies have a team for that.

"As a service" means you don't build this team. Somebody else runs the monitoring and the analysis and sends you the result on a schedule. This is what we do with Star Track: we do the collecting and the analysis, you get a report, and your team stays on the product.

How do I find out who my competitors are?

The usual way now is to ask ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. It is fast, but their data can be outdated, and they mostly know the companies everybody already knows. If you are a small business, the ones that matter are at your scale and often working silently. They don't show up there.

Some tools go one step further. They keep a database of websites and search through vector embeddings to find the ones similar to yours. The idea is good, but the result depends completely on how big and how fresh the database is.

We took a different road. We monitor the listing websites where startups announce themselves, such as Product Hunt, G2 and AlternativeTo, plus more than 100 smaller directories, and we built one big dataset of domains from them. Today it holds more than 300,000 unique domains, one for each startup, which is bigger than every other tool we have compared it with.

Next to it runs an active crawling system and an explorer we developed, which uses the existing data to discover and index new startups on the internet. So when we look for your competitors, we search in all of that, not in what a model happens to remember.

How can I track my competitors' pricing changes?

You need to check their pricing pages and subscription plans actively, because nobody announces a price change. You can do it manually: open each website, write down the plans, the limits and the prices, and come back in a few weeks to compare. For three competitors it is fine. For thirty it becomes frustrating and takes hours, and you will still miss the change which happened between two visits.

We do this checking for you on many websites similar to your startup, and we keep the history. What you get is a fresh and polished analysis built from the aggregated data: where the price range of your market is, who moved up or down, and what they changed in their plans. With that you understand your own pricing much better than from one competitor's page.

How often should a startup do competitor analysis?

Every month, in our opinion. Because of the AI boom, tons of startups are created every day, and the market changes its shape rapidly, month by month. A yearly or even quarterly analysis is old before you finish reading it.

Monthly also fits how product teams work. If you plan in sprints, a fresh market analysis each month shows you the new ideas that came out and the changes around you, just in time to plan the next ones.

The hard part is the cost. Monitoring so many companies without proper tooling is expensive, and this is the reason most small teams do it once and never again.

What should a competitor analysis report include?

Anything that can measure a business. At minimum we would look for:

  • Hiring and the size of the team
  • Product changes and new features
  • Prices and plans
  • The market and the countries they work in
  • Social media engagement
  • Number of sales, when they report it
  • Blog posts and website updates
  • Website traffic and the number of backlinks

One number alone tells little. Hiring three engineers together with a new pricing page tells you a launch is coming. That is why a good report puts these factors next to each other and says what changed since the last time.

How much should competitor analysis cost for a small company?

There are many solutions on the internet and they sit at two ends. The expensive ones are built for enterprises. A small or medium company, especially in its early stage, can't afford them consistently, and even then the data may contain noise or outdated information.

The cheap ones cover only a few of the factors, so they don't give you the required information or a real vision of your competitors.

We built Star Track for the gap between them: the full set of factors, at a price a small team can keep paying. A single report costs $99.

What is the difference between market research and business intelligence?

Market research answers a question you ask once: is there demand, who are the customers, how big is the market. It is usually a project with a start and an end, done with surveys, interviews and reports.

Business intelligence is continuous. It is about collecting data all the time and watching how it changes, so you can make the daily decisions with it. Traditionally it meant your internal data, like sales and usage. What we do is apply the same idea to the outside: your competitors and your market, monitored continuously.

In practice a startup needs both. Research at the beginning to choose the direction, and intelligence afterwards to not lose it.
